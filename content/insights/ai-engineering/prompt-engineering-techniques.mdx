<InsightSummary>
Prompt engineering is not magic words — it is a small set of reliable techniques for telling a model exactly what you want. Zero-shot for simple tasks, few-shot to show the format, role and instruction prompting to set expertise and constraints, chain-of-thought for multi-step problems, and output formatting plus delimiters to get usable, structured results. Each is simple on its own; knowing which to reach for is the actual skill. The common thread: the goal is not any answer, it is an answer you can actually *use*.
</InsightSummary>

<Body>
## Zero-shot

Just ask, with no examples. It fits small, clear-cut jobs like a fast fact, a short summary, a plain explanation, or a rewrite. Example: *"Give me the main point of this email in two sentences."* If the task is unambiguous, zero-shot is the cheapest path and usually enough.

## Few-shot

Give a handful of input→output examples before the real task, so the model infers the pattern and format. Example:

```
Review: "Arrived broken"    -> Label: negative
Review: "Works great"       -> Label: positive
Review: "Fast and reliable" -> Label:
```

Two to five examples is the sweet spot. Reach for few-shot when you need a specific output *format* or labeling scheme that is hard to describe in words but easy to demonstrate.

## Role prompting

Tell the model who it is supposed to be. Example: *"You are an experienced database engineer. Explain indexing to a junior developer on your team."* It works because one short role line sets how the answer sounds, how expert it is, how deep it goes, and how it is laid out — far more efficient than spelling out each of those separately.

## Instruction prompting

Spell out the task in plain, exact terms. What separates a weak prompt from a strong one is how precise it is:

- **Weak:** *"Write a product description."*
- **Strong:** *"Write a product description under 100 words for busy parents. Keep the tone warm, use two short paragraphs, and finish with one call to action."*

The strong version pins down length, reader, tone, and layout, so the model has far less to guess. Add background with **context injection** (*"I am new to SQL. Show me how a JOIN works with a tiny example."*); the more relevant background you give, the better the answer fits.

## Chain-of-thought (CoT)

Ask the model to work through the problem in order instead of jumping to an answer. Say "think step by step," or list the steps yourself:

```
This API call times out. Work through it in order:
1. Describe what is failing
2. Explain why it fails
3. Propose a change
```

Use it when a problem has several stages, such as tracking down a bug, solving a math or logic puzzle, designing a system, making a plan, or finding why something broke. One practical note for work settings: ask for *short* reasoning steps, not a long stream of thinking. You want the model to reason, not ramble.

## Output formatting and delimiters

Control the *shape* of the answer so it is easy to consume downstream:

- **Output formatting:** *"Answer as a two-column table," "Reply with JSON only, using these keys," "Give me three bullets and nothing else."* A predictable shape is much easier to feed into code, scripts, reports, and other tools.
- **Delimiters:** put the input inside clear markers (e.g. triple quotes or XML-style tags) so the model can tell your *instructions* apart from the *data* it should work on: *"Translate only the text between the tags into French."* The model then cannot mistake part of the content for a command, and answers get more accurate, especially with long documents, call transcripts, source code, and emails.

## Weak vs strong, in one line

The pattern behind every technique is the same: a weak prompt leaves the model guessing; a strong prompt removes the guesswork. *"Write something about AI"* invites a generic essay; *"Explain RAG to a non-technical manager in 5 bullet points, each under 15 words"* gets you exactly what you can use. The point was never just to get a reply. It was to get one you can put to work.
</Body>

<KeyTakeaways>
- Zero-shot (no examples) suits simple, well-defined tasks; few-shot (2–5 examples) teaches the model an output format you can't easily describe.
- A role line shapes voice, expertise, and depth at once; instruction prompting and context injection remove ambiguity by stating constraints and background explicitly.
- Chain-of-thought makes the model reason step by step for multi-step problems; at work, ask for short steps rather than a long monologue.
- Output formatting (tables, JSON, bullets) and delimiters (separating instructions from data) turn raw answers into usable, accurate ones.
- The unifying rule: weak prompts make the model guess; strong, specific prompts remove the guesswork.
</KeyTakeaways>
