## The 5-sentence cold email formula

Every effective cold email has exactly these 5 elements:

1. **Specific hook** — why you are emailing *this person* (not a generic greeting)
2. **Who you are** — one sentence, relevant context only
3. **What you built** — one specific project or achievement that is relevant to their work
4. **The ask** — one clear, small ask (not "give me a job")
5. **Easy out** — make it effortless to say no

Total length: 5-7 sentences. Under 150 words. The recipient should be able to read and decide in 30 seconds.

## Template 1: Cold email to an engineer at a target company

```
Subject: Quick question about [Company]'s [specific team/product]

Hi [First Name],

I saw your talk at [event] / read your blog post about [topic] /
noticed your work on [project] — [one specific thing you found
interesting or learned from it].

I'm [Name], a CS student at [College] graduating in [Year]. I
recently built [project name] — a [one-sentence description] —
and the [specific technical challenge] connects to what your
team works on.

Would you have 15 minutes for a quick call about what it's like
working on [team/product]? I'd love to learn what problems the
team is tackling right now.

Totally understand if you're too busy — appreciate you reading
this either way.

Best,
[Name]
[GitHub] | [LinkedIn] | [Project link]
```

**Why this works:**
- Opens with something specific about *them*, not about you
- Shows you did homework (their talk, blog, or product)
- Links your project to their work
- Asks for a conversation, not a job
- Makes it easy to say no

## Template 2: LinkedIn DM to a recruiter

```
Hi [Name],

I came across the [Role] opening at [Company] and I'm very
interested. I'm a [Year] CS grad from [College] with experience
in [2-3 relevant skills].

My most relevant project is [name] — [one sentence + link].
I think it aligns with what the [Team] is building.

Would it be okay to send you my resume? Happy to share more
context on my background.

Thank you,
[Name]
```

**Key principles:**
- Do not send your resume without asking — it feels pushy
- Mention the specific role and team, not "any openings"
- Keep it under 80 words for LinkedIn DMs

## Template 3: Referral request to a college senior or alumni

```
Subject: [College] [Year] — asking about your experience at [Company]

Hi [Name],

I'm [Your Name], currently in [year] at [College] — I found your
profile through the [College] alumni network / LinkedIn.

I'm applying for [Role] at [Company] and I've been working on
[relevant project / skill]. I'd love to hear about your experience
there and whether you think my background could be a fit.

If you're open to it, would a 15-minute call work sometime this
week? I can also share my resume and GitHub for context.

Thanks for considering — I know your time is valuable.

[Name]
```

**Why alumni outreach has the highest reply rate:**
- Shared college creates immediate affinity
- Most alumni remember being in your position
- Many companies have referral bonuses, so it benefits them too

## Template 4: Follow-up after no reply (send after 5-7 days)

```
Subject: Re: [Original subject line]

Hi [Name],

Just bumping this in case it got buried. I understand you're busy
— if now isn't a good time, no worries at all.

[One new piece of context: "I just shipped [feature] on the project
I mentioned" or "I saw [Company] announced [thing] — very relevant
to my work on [project]."]

Either way, thanks for your time.

[Name]
```

**Rules for follow-ups:**
- Send exactly one follow-up, 5-7 days later
- Add something new — do not just repeat the original message
- Never send more than 2 messages total (original + 1 follow-up)
- Never guilt-trip: "I haven't heard back..." is passive-aggressive

## Finding the right people to email

### For startups:
- **CTO or Engineering Manager** — at startups under 100 people, they often make hiring decisions directly
- Find their email via the company website's team page, or use LinkedIn

### For product companies (Razorpay, Zerodha, etc.):
- **Engineers on the team you want to join** — they can refer you internally
- Find them via LinkedIn, company tech blogs, or conference speaker lists

### For any company:
- **College alumni** — search `[Company] [College] LinkedIn` and filter by current employees
- **Recruiters who posted the job listing** — they are literally looking for candidates

### Email finding tools:
- Company email patterns are usually `firstname@company.com` or `firstname.lastname@company.com`
- Check the company's GitHub organization — commit emails are often visible
- Use LinkedIn "Contact Info" section if connected

## The 7 messages that always get ignored

1. **"Dear Sir/Madam"** — tells them you did not bother to find their name
2. **"I am a passionate and hardworking individual"** — empty words
3. **"Please find attached my resume"** as the entire message — no context, no connection
4. **500-word life story** — nobody reads this
5. **"Any openings at your company?"** — do your research, name the role
6. **Mass-copied message sent to 50 people** — people can tell
7. **"I would be grateful for the opportunity to prove myself"** — desperation is not persuasive

<CommonMistakes>
- **Sending the same message to 50 people** — personalization takes 5 minutes per message. 5 personalized emails will outperform 50 generic ones every time.
- **Asking for a job in the first message** — ask for a conversation. "Could we chat for 15 minutes?" is much more likely to get a yes than "Are you hiring?"
- **Not including a link to your work** — your message claims you built something impressive. Prove it with a link. No link = no credibility.
- **Writing a novel** — if your email is longer than the screen on a phone, it will not be read. 5-7 sentences maximum.
- **Never following up** — one polite follow-up is expected. Not following up leaves potential replies on the table. But more than one follow-up crosses into spam.
- **Emailing only HR** — HR manages the process but rarely champions candidates. Email engineers and managers who understand your technical work.
</CommonMistakes>
