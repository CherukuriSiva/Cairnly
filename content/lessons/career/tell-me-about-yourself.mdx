<WhyThisMatters>
"Tell me about yourself" is the first question in 90%+ of interviews. A rambling, unfocused answer in the first 90 seconds sets a negative tone for the entire interview. A structured answer does three things: it shows communication skills, it guides the interviewer toward your strengths, and it sets up follow-up questions you are prepared for.
</WhyThisMatters>

<Body>
## The Present-Past-Future structure

This is not a memorized speech. It is a 3-part framework you fill in differently for each interview.

### Part 1: Present (15-20 seconds)

What you are doing right now. Lead with your most relevant current identity.

> "I'm a final-year CS student at NIT Trichy, currently building a RAG-based document search tool as my capstone project and interning part-time at a fintech startup where I work on their payments API."

### Part 2: Past (20-30 seconds)

One or two experiences that are relevant to *this specific role*. Do not recite your entire resume.

> "Last summer I interned at Razorpay on the merchant onboarding team, where I built an internal tool that reduced KYC verification time by 35%. Before that, I got into backend development through a full-stack project — a multi-tenant task management app — which taught me how to think about database design and API contracts."

### Part 3: Future (15-20 seconds)

Why you are here, specifically. Connect your past to this company and role.

> "I'm drawn to [Company] because your search infrastructure team works on the kind of retrieval problems I've been exploring in my capstone. I want to go deeper on distributed systems and work at a scale where design decisions actually matter."

## Adapting for different company types

### For startups (Razorpay, CRED, Zerodha)

Emphasize: breadth, ownership, shipping speed, willingness to wear multiple hats.

> "...I like small teams where I can own a feature end-to-end. At my internship, I went from Figma to production in two weeks."

### For big tech (Google, Microsoft, Amazon India)

Emphasize: scale, systems thinking, specific technical depth.

> "...I'm interested in building systems that handle millions of requests. My capstone project pushed me to think about caching and indexing strategies at scale."

### For service/consulting (TCS Digital, Infosys Power Programmer)

Emphasize: adaptability, learning speed, breadth of tech exposure.

> "...I've worked across the stack — React frontends, Python backends, and basic DevOps. I pick up new technologies fast, which I showed when I learned Go in two weeks for a side project."

## The 5 mistakes to avoid

### 1. Starting with "I'm a passionate developer..."

Everyone says this. It communicates nothing. Start with a concrete fact about what you are doing right now.

### 2. Reciting your resume line by line

The interviewer has your resume. They want a *narrative*, not a reading. Pick 1-2 highlights that connect to the role.

### 3. Going over 90 seconds

Practice with a timer. If you hit 2 minutes, you are rambling. The interviewer's eyes have glazed over.

### 4. Not connecting to the specific company

"I want to work at a good company" is generic. Name something specific about the company: a product, a team, a tech stack choice, a blog post.

### 5. Ending without energy

Do not trail off with "...so yeah, that's about it." End with a clear, forward-looking statement: "I'm excited about the systems engineering challenges on your team and I'd love to dig into the details."

## Practice template

Fill in these blanks and practice aloud 3 times:

```
[PRESENT] I'm a [year] CS student at [college]. Right now I'm
[most relevant current activity — project, internship, or research].

[PAST] Previously, I [one relevant experience + specific result].
That's where I got deep into [skill/area relevant to this role].

[FUTURE] I'm interviewing at [Company] because [specific reason
tied to the role]. I want to [growth goal that aligns with the
team's work].
```

Time yourself. Aim for 60-90 seconds.
