<Body>
## Start with the numbers

Pin the targets before designing: expected RPS, p95 latency budget, payload size, and whether it's GPU- or CPU-bound. "p95 < 300ms at 1000 RPS" turns a vague question into an engineering problem you can reason about.

## The request path

**Gateway/LB** → **semantic cache** (return hits instantly) → **inference workers** (stateless, model loaded once, behind autoscaling) → **response**. Cross-cutting: a **model registry** feeds workers their versioned model; **monitoring** watches latency/errors/drift.

## The levers, and when to pull each

- **Semantic cache** — kills latency and cost on repeat/near-duplicate queries. First lever.
- **Micro-batching** — pack concurrent requests into one model call for throughput.
- **Horizontal autoscaling** — stateless workers, scale on concurrency/p95, readiness probes for warm-up.
- **Quantization** — INT8/FP16 to fit more per accelerator and cut latency, gated on an eval check.
- **Provider/replica fallback + circuit breakers** — for availability under partial failure.

## Capacity math, out loud

"If one worker holds ~50 RPS at the latency budget, 1000 RPS needs ~20 workers plus headroom; autoscale 5→30 on concurrency." Showing the arithmetic is the senior move.

## Close with observability

Audit/structured logs, p50/p95/p99 dashboards, drift monitors, and load-test-derived capacity numbers. A design without monitoring is a prototype.
</Body>

<CommonMistakes>
- **Designing without targets** — pin RPS and p95 first
- **Stateful workers** — breaks horizontal scaling
- **Forgetting the cache** — the cheapest latency/cost win goes unmentioned
- **Quantizing without an eval gate** — silent accuracy loss
- **No capacity arithmetic** — naming components isn't sizing a system
- **No monitoring/fallback** — reads as a prototype, not production
</CommonMistakes>
